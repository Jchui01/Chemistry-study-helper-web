Topic 1

Fundamentals of Chemistry & Planet Earth

Classification of Matter, Lab Safety, The Atmosphere, The Ocean, Rocks & Minerals, and The Lime Cycle.

1.1 Matter & Lab Safety 1.2 The Atmosphere 1.3 The Ocean 1.4 Rocks & Minerals

Element

Pure substance that cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical methods (1 kind of atom, e.g., Na, H2, O2, Br2, Si).

Compound

Pure substance of 2+ elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio (sharp m.p./b.p.; separated only by chemical methods, e.g., H2O, NaCl).

Mixture

2+ pure substances NOT chemically combined (variable composition; melts/boils over a range; components retain own properties; e.g., air, sea water, steel).

Abundance (Must Memorize): Earth's crust by mass: O (50%) > Si (26%) > Al (7%) > Fe (4%). Elements at 25 °C, 1 atm: 2 liquids (Hg, Br2); 11 gases (6 noble gases + H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2); all others solids.

Physical vs. Chemical Changes: Physical = no new substance (melting, sublimation, distillation, dissolution). Chemical = new substance(s) formed (combustion, rusting, decomposition, neutralization).

Bunsen Burner & Hazards: Close air hole before lighting to prevent striking back (open air hole → hot, blue, non-luminous, noisy flame). Key hazards: Flammable (H2, ethanol, K/Na), Oxidizing (conc. HNO3/H2SO4, KMnO4), Toxic (Cl2, CO, NO2, SO2), Corrosive (conc. acids/alkalis), Irritant (dilute acids/alkalis).

Composition of Dry Air (by volume): N2 (78%) > O2 (21%) > Ar (0.93%) > CO2 (0.03%) > other noble gases (0.04%).

Fractional Distillation of Liquefied Air: (1) Purify air (filter dust; remove CO2 & H2O vapour as solids). (2) Cool to −200 °C by repeated compression & rapid expansion to liquefy. (3) Warm slowly in fractionating column → boil-off order: N2 (−196 °C) → Ar (−186 °C) → O2 (−183 °C).

Key Uses & Test: N2 (NH3 fertilizers, inert food packaging, liquid N2 cooling); O2 (respiration, supports combustion — oxidizing, NOT flammable; test: relights a glowing splint); He (balloons — inert & lighter than air); Ne (red signs); Ar (light bulbs — prevents tungsten oxidation).

Sea Water Extraction: Filtration removes insoluble sand. Evaporation to dryness gives impure NaCl powder (contains MgCl2 etc.). Crystallization (heat to saturated → cool slowly → filter & wash w/ little cold distilled water) gives large, pure crystals. Simple distillation isolates pure water: use anti-bumping granules (smooth boiling), enter cooling water at the bottom of the condenser, and keep receiver open (prevent explosion).

Reactions & Tests of Calcium Carbonate (CaCO3)

• Insoluble in water. Flame test for Ca2+: brick-red flame.

• + Acid (dil. HCl / HNO3, NOT H2SO4 due to insoluble CaSO4 coating): CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g) (effervescence).

• Thermal decomposition: CaCO3(s) —(heat)→ CaO(s) + CO2(g).

Atomic number (Z) = no. of p+ (= no. of e− in neutral atom).

Mass number (A) = no. of p+ + no. of n (always integer). Thus n = A − Z.

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element with the same no. of p+ but different no. of n. They have SAME chemical properties (same electronic arrangement) but DIFFERENT physical properties (different mass, m.p., b.p.).

Relative Atomic Mass (R.A.M.)

Weighted average isotopic mass of all natural isotopes on the 12C = 12.00 scale (no unit).

R.A.M. = Σ (% abundance × relative isotopic mass)

Max e− per shell = 2n2 (1st: 2, 2nd: 8, 3rd: max 18, usually 8 first).

Arranged in order of increasing atomic number (NOT R.A.M., e.g., 18Ar 40.0 before 19K 39.1; 27Co before 28Ni). Period no. = occupied electron shells. Group no. = outermost shell e− (except He). Hydrogen belongs to NO group.

Across a Period (Left → Right): Metals → Metalloids (B, Si) → Non-metals (metallic character decreases). Melting point rises Gp I → III (stronger metallic bonds), peaks at Gp IV (giant covalent C, Si), drops low for Gp V–VII (simple molecular), and is lowest for Gp 0 (monoatomic).

Ionic Bond & Naming

Transfer of e− from metal to non-metal → strong non-directional electrostatic attraction between cations & anions.

⚠️ Exception: Ammonium salts (NH4Cl, (NH4)2SO4) are ionic compounds containing NO metals (covalent inside NH4+, ionic between NH4+ & anion).

Naming reminder: -ide = no O (except OH− hydroxide): nitride N3−, sulphide S2−. -ite vs -ate: nitrite NO2− / nitrate NO3−; sulphite SO32− / sulphate SO42−.

Covalent, Dative & Metallic Bonds

Covalent: Sharing e− between non-metals (bond pairs & lone pairs). Prefixes: mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-.

Dative Covalent (→): BOTH shared e− donated by the same atom. Key DSE Examples: NH4+ (NH3 + H+), H3O+ (H2O + H+), H3N→BF3 (B attains octet), BF4−, CO, O3, HNO3, Al2Cl6.

Metallic: Strong non-directional electrostatic attraction between lattice of positive metal cations and 'sea' of delocalized e−.

Key Structural Points

• Metallic strength: Across period Na < Mg < Al (more outermost e− & smaller atomic size). Down group K < Na < Li (larger atomic size).

• Ionic lattices: NaCl is 6:6; CsCl is 8:8 (Cs+ is larger than Na+). Brittle because shifting layers brings like charges together → repulsion shatters lattice.

• Simple Molecular b.p. trend: Larger molecular size → stronger van der Waals' → higher b.p. (F2 < Cl2 < Br2 < I2; He < Ne < Ar; CH4 < C2H6; CH4 < SiH4).

Diamond vs. Graphite vs. SiO2

• Diamond: Each C tetrahedrally bonded to 4 C (109.5°); all 4 outer e− localized → very hard, non-conductor.

• Graphite: Hexagonal layers; each C bonded to 3 C → 1 delocalized e− per C conducts electricity (electrodes). Weak van der Waals' between layers → layers slide & flake off (pencils, solid lubricant).

• SiO2 (quartz): Each Si bonded to 4 O; each O bonded to 2 Si (1:2 ratio).

📝 DSE Answering Frameworks (Score Full Marks!)

Conductivity Golden Rule: Requires EITHER (1) delocalized/mobile electrons (metals s/l; graphite s) OR (2) mobile ions (ionic compounds l/aq; acids/alkalis aq).

1. Why NaCl (or KI) has higher b.p. than CO2 (or HI) [2M]: NaCl has a giant ionic structure with strong ionic bonds between Na+ and Cl− ions, while CO2 has a simple molecular structure with weak van der Waals' forces between CO2 molecules. More heat energy is needed to overcome the strong ionic bonds.

2. Why Diamond (or SiO2) has higher b.p. than CO2 (or O2) [2M]: Diamond has a giant covalent structure with strong covalent bonds between C atoms, while CO2 has a simple molecular structure with weak van der Waals' forces between CO2 molecules.

3. Period 3 Oxides Molten Conductivity (2012 DSE): Given Na2O, MgO, Al2O3, SiO2, P4O10, SO2, Cl2O → Only Na2O, MgO, Al2O3 conduct electricity when molten because they are ionic compounds containing mobile ions in the molten state.

Water, Steam & Typical Dilute Acids

• Cold water (K, Na, Ca): Metal + H2O(l) → Metal hydroxide + H2(g).

• Steam (Mg, Al, Zn, Fe): Metal + H2O(g) → Metal oxide + H2(g).

• Typical acids (dil. HCl, dil. H2SO4, CH3COOH, v.dil. HNO3): Metal (above Cu) + acid → Salt + H2(g). No reaction for Cu, Hg, Ag, Pt, Au.

Displacement Reactions (Exothermic)

• Metals: More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal from aq/molten salt (must have mobile ions): Mg(s) + CuSO4(aq) → MgSO4(aq) + Cu(s).

• Halogens: Reactivity Cl2 > Br2 > I2: Cl2(g) + 2KBr(aq) → 2KCl(aq) + Br2(aq).

2 Necessary Conditions: Both O2(g) and H2O(l) must be present. Rust formula: Fe2O3·xH2O(s) (hydrated iron(III) oxide).

Step 1: Fe(s) → Fe2+(aq) + 2e−   |   O2(g) + 2H2O(l) + 4e− → 4OH−(aq)
Overall: 4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) + 2xH2O(l) → 2Fe2O3·xH2O(s)

Rust Indicator: K3Fe(CN)6(aq) turns yellow → blue (detects Fe2+); phenolphthalein turns colourless → pink (detects OH−).

1. Protective Coating

  • Paint / Grease: Paint for large objects; oil/grease for moving machine parts.
  • Galvanising (Zn): Excludes air/water + Zn forms tight ZnO + sacrificial protection.
  • Tin-plating (Sn): Used on food cans (Zn is toxic).
  • Electroplating (Cr): Shiny silvery look (water taps).

2. Sacrificial Protection

Connect iron to a more reactive metal (Mg or Zn blocks on ship hulls / underground pipes). The more reactive metal loses e− preferentially, suppressing Fe → Fe2+ + 2e−.

Car body: Connected to the negative terminal of the car battery to supply e− and retard rusting.

3. Alloying

Stainless steel: Iron alloyed with carbon, chromium, and nickel (plus Mn) — highly resistant to rusting.

⚠️ Exam Trap (Tin-Plating): If a tin-plated food can is scratched, the exposed iron rusts FASTER than ordinary iron because Fe is more reactive than Sn and loses electrons more readily!

Empirical Formula (EF) vs. Molecular Formula (MF)

• EF: Simplest whole-number ratio of atoms (e.g., CH3 for C2H6).

• MF: Actual number of atoms in 1 molecule (simple molecular only). MF = (EF)n, where n = Molar Mass ÷ EF Mass.

• Steps: Mass (or % assuming 100 g) → ÷ R.A.M. → ÷ smallest moles → convert to whole-number ratio.

Stoichiometry: A balanced chemical equation gives the mole ratio (NEVER the mass ratio). Law of Conservation of Mass: Total mass of reactants consumed = total mass of products formed.

Limiting Reactant Rule

Divide each reactant's moles by its stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation. The reactant giving the smaller value is the limiting reactant and determines the amount of product formed. Excess left = initial moles − moles consumed.

Percentage Yield

% Yield = (Actual yield ÷ Theoretical yield) × 100%

4 Reasons Actual < Theoretical: (1) Incomplete reaction, (2) Side-reactions, (3) Impurities in reactants, (4) Loss of product during experiment.

Basicity (Max no. of H+ per acid molecule)

• Monobasic (1): HCl, HNO3, HNO2, HCOOH (not 2!), CH3COOH (not 4!), HOCl.

Reactions of Acids

• + Metals (>Cu): → Salt + H2(g) ('pop' sound w/ burning splint).

• + Metal oxide / hydroxide / NH3: Neutralization → Salt + H2O (no H2O shown for NH3).

• + Sulphites / HSO3−: → Salt + H2O + SO2(g).

Concentrated Acids & Dilution Safety

• Conc. HCl (~11M): Corrosive (high [H+]), non-oxidizing, white fumes.

• Conc. HNO3 (~16M): Corrosive mainly due to oxidizing property (reacts w/ Cu, Ag → brown NO2).

• Conc. H2SO4 (~18M, (l)): Corrosive due to dehydrating (blue CuSO4·5H2O → white CuSO4; glucose/sucrose → black C + H2O) & oxidizing (→ SO2) properties.

Diluting Conc. H2SO4: Add acid slowly to a large amount of water with constant stirring (never water to acid → exothermic splashing!).

Drying Agents & Inverted Funnel

• Anhydrous CaCl2(s): Dries all gases EXCEPT NH3(g).

• CaO(s): Dries neutral & alkaline gases (e.g., NH3).

• Conc. H2SO4(l): Dries neutral & acidic gases (HCl, SO2, Cl2, CO2).

• Inverted funnel: Used to dissolve very soluble gases (HCl, NH3, SO2) in water to prevent sucking back.

• 5-Step Ionic Equations: Balance full eq → add states → split soluble ionic/strong acids → cancel spectator ions → check atoms & total charge.

Base: Reacts with acid → salt + water only (metal oxides, hydroxides, NH3). Alkali: Water-soluble base giving OH−(aq) (Gp I/Ba/Ca oxides & hydroxides, NH3). Alkaline substances (Na2CO3, NaHCO3) give pH > 7 but are not alkalis.

⭐ Must-Memorize Solubility Summary

• Soluble in excess NaOH(aq): Zn(OH)2, Pb(OH)2, Al(OH)3 (remember ZPA).

• Soluble in excess NH3(aq): Zn(OH)2, Cu(OH)2, Ag2O (remember ZCA).

• Insoluble in BOTH: Mg(OH)2, Fe(OH)2, Fe(OH)3.

Ammonium Salts & Lab Prep of Dry NH3(g)

Base + Ammonium salt —(heat)→ Salt + H2O + NH3(g)

• Heat NH4Cl(s) + CaO(s) in a downward-tilted boiling tube (prevents condensed water cracking hot tube). Dry with CaO(s). Collect by upward delivery (molar mass 17 < air 28.8) or gas syringe. Test: turns moist red litmus paper blue.

Thermal Decomposition, Non-Metal Oxides & Safety

• Heating hydroxides (Mn+ ≥ 2): → Metal oxide + H2O(g) (e.g., blue Cu(OH)2 → black CuO + H2O). Ag2O → Ag + O2.

• + CO2 / SO2: Forms carbonate/sulphite; excess forms soluble HCO3− / HSO3−.

• Conc. alkali spill: Wash with large amount of cold running water (NEVER neutralize on skin w/ vinegar → exothermic burns!).

Molarity, Dilution & Mixing

Molarity (M) = mol ÷ dm3  |  g dm−3 = M × Molar Mass

• Density + % by mass (e.g. 2014 DSE): 1.83 g cm−3, 94% H2SO4 → in 1 cm3: mass = 1.72 g, mol = 0.0175 → M = 0.0175 ÷ 0.001 = 17.5 M.

• Dilution: M1V1 = M2V2 (moles unchanged).

• Mixing solutions: Final M = (n1 + n2) ÷ (V1 + V2). ⚠️ NEVER use M1V1 = M2V2 for mixing!

pH Scale (0–14 at 25 °C)

pH = −log[H+(aq)]  |  [H+][OH−] = 10−14 M2

• Change of 1 pH unit = 10-fold change in [H+]. Acidity depends on [H+] (conc.), NOT moles or volume.

• Measuring pH: Universal indicator, pH paper, pH meter (accurate), pH data logger (continuous & accurate). Litmus, methyl orange, and phenolphthalein CANNOT measure exact pH!

Strength vs. Concentration & 3 Factors Affecting Acid pH

• Strong (100% ionize/dissociate): HCl, HNO3, H2SO4; NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)2. Weak (<100%): CH3COOH, H2CO3, H2SO3, HOCl; NH3, Ca(OH)2. Strength ≠ Concentration!

• Comparing Strengths (same conc.): Strong acid/alkali has more extreme pH, higher electrical conductivity (bulb glows brighter), and faster reaction rate with Mg / CaCO3.

• 3 Factors Affecting Acid pH: (1) Concentration (0.1 M HCl < 0.01 M HCl), (2) Strength (0.1 M HCl < 0.1 M CH3COOH), (3) Basicity (0.1 M H2SO4 [0.2 M H+] < 0.1 M HCl [0.1 M H+]).

Exothermic Nature & Temperature Rise

• Heat released depends on: (1) Strength (SA+SB > SA+WB ≈ WA+SB > WA+WB, as weak acids/alkalis absorb heat to ionize); (2) Moles of H2O formed.

• Temperature rise depends on: Strength + ratio (moles of H2O formed ÷ total solution volume).

Applications of Neutralization

• Soil pH: CaO / Ca(OH)2 / CaCO3 (slightly soluble, won't make soil too alkaline; NaOH/KOH too alkaline; NH3 toxic/volatile). Alkaline soil → (NH4)2SO4 / NH4NO3.

• Antacids: Mg(OH)2, Al(OH)3 preferred over carbonates (CO2 causes stomach discomfort).

• Waste & Fertilizers: Na2CO3 (faster than Ca(OH)2) for acid waste; H2SO4 for alkaline waste; NH4NO3 & (NH4)2SO4 for N-fertilizers.

Normal vs. Acid Salts: Normal salt = all ionizable H replaced (Na2SO4, Na3PO4). Acid salt = still contains ionizable H (NaHSO4, NaHCO3, NaH2PO4). pH of salt solutions: SA+SB → neutral (pH 7); SA+WB → acidic (pH < 7, e.g. NH4Cl); WA+SB → alkaline (pH > 7, e.g. CH3COONa, Na2CO3). Note: Acid salts NaHCO3 and NaHSO3 are actually ALKALINE!

Type 1: Insoluble Salt

e.g., AgCl, BaSO4, PbCl2, CuCO3

Precipitation: Mix 2 aqueous solutions (if solid, dissolve first) → stir → filter off ppt → wash with distilled water → dry between filter papers or desiccator.

Type 2: Soluble (Non-Na/K/NH4+)

e.g., MgCl2, ZnSO4, CuCl2

Acid + EXCESS insoluble metal/oxide/hydroxide/carbonate (ensures no acid left; note Cu cannot react w/ HCl!) → filter unreacted solid → heat filtrate to saturated → cool slowly → filter, wash w/ little cold distilled water → dry (do NOT heat hydrated crystals!).

Type 3: Soluble (Na+, K+, NH4+)

e.g., NaCl, KNO3, NH4NO3, NaHSO4

Titration / Neutralization: Mix exact mole ratio. If conc. unknown: titrate V1 alkali w/ indicator to find V2 acid → remix V1 & V2 WITHOUT indicator → crystallize.

Standard Solution (Known Molarity)

• 5 Requirements for Primary Standard: (1) Highly pure, (2) Stable in air/solution, (3) Does not absorb H2O or CO2, (4) Soluble in water, (5) Large molar mass (minimizes % weighing error). E.g., anhydrous Na2CO3, (COOH)2·2H2O.

• Unsuitable (must standardize!): Conc. H2SO4 (hygroscopic), NaOH/KOH (hygroscopic + absorbs CO2 → Na2CO3), NH3/Br2 (volatile), HCl (gas).

• From Solid: Weigh by difference → dissolve in beaker → transfer + washings via funnel to volumetric flask → top up to mark w/ dropper → stopper & invert.

Thermometric Titration & Calculations

• Equivalence point: Exact stoichiometric reaction. End point: Sharp physical change observed.

• Temperature graph: Rises (exothermic) → peaks at end point → drops (excess cold titrant added). Use plastic cup (insulator). Data logger advantages: More accurate/sensitive, instant graphs/tables, saves time (NO mark for "more convenient").

• Titration Calcs: Ignore 1st rough/outlier titre; remember dilution factor (e.g., 250/25 = ×10); diluting sample saves chemicals.

🔁 Back Titration (5** High-Yield Topic)

Used when: (1) Reactant is an insoluble solid / reaction is too slow, OR (2) No suitable indicator exists.

Moles of X reacted with Y = Total excess moles of X − Moles of X left (found by titrating with Z)

  • Mg or CaCO3 + excess HCl → back-titrate with NaOH: mol(Mg or CaCO3) = [mol(HCl total) − mol(NaOH)] ÷ 2.
  • Ammonium salt + excess KOH → back-titrate with HCl: Warm mixture to drive off NH3(g) completely so NH3 won't react with HCl!
  • Aspirin + excess NaOH (heat, 1:2 ratio) → back-titrate with HCl: mol(aspirin) = [mol(NaOH total) − mol(HCl)] ÷ 2.
  • Office Paper CaCO3 % (2 Methods): (1) Immerse in excess HCl until no bubbles → wash, dry at 110 °C, reweigh (mass loss = CaCO3). (2) Immerse in known excess HCl → back-titrate excess HCl with standard NaOH.
  • Antacid Al(OH)3 tablet: Dissolve in known excess HCl → dilute to 250 cm3 → titrate 25 cm3 aliquot with NaOH (remember ×10 factor & 1:3 ratio!).
